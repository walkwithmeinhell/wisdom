1. DATA MINING AND MACHINE LEARNING OVERVIEW
============================================================

Statistics versus data mining
- Traditional statistical work often focuses on explaining or describing population relationships, fitting interpretable models, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and p-values.
- Data mining often focuses on prediction for new records, larger datasets, many variables, predictive performance, and decision costs.
- These are not mutually exclusive; the emphasis differs.

Core data-mining ideas
- Classification
- Prediction
- Association rules and recommender systems
- Data and dimension reduction
- Data exploration
- Visualisation

Common process frameworks
SEMMA:
- Sample
- Explore
- Modify
- Model
- Assess

CRISP-DM:
- Business understanding
- Data understanding
- Data preparation
- Modelling
- Evaluation
- Deployment

Supervised learning
- The goal is to predict one target or outcome variable.
- Training data contain known target values.
- The fitted model is then used to score records whose target value is not known.
- Classification predicts a categorical outcome.
- Prediction predicts a numerical outcome.

Unsupervised learning
- There is no target variable.
- The goal is to find patterns or meaningful groupings.
- Examples include clustering, association rules, data reduction and exploratory visualisation.

Classification
- Predicts a categorical target.
- Examples include purchase/no purchase, fraud/no fraud, and creditworthy/not creditworthy.
- The target is often binary, but classification can be multiclass.

Prediction
- Predicts a numerical target such as sales, revenue, cost or performance.
- Classification and numerical prediction are both forms of predictive analytics.

Association rules
- Look for "what goes with what".
- Commonly use transaction-style data.
- Can support recommender systems.
- Also called affinity analysis.

Data reduction
- Reduces complexity.
- May reduce the number of variables, for example with principal components.
- May reduce or summarise records, for example by clustering.

Data exploration
- Real datasets are often large, complex and messy.
- Exploration helps understand the data and refine the analytical task.
- Histograms, boxplots, bar charts and scatterplots are common tools.

============================================================
2. DATA PREPARATION
============================================================

Variable types
- Main distinction: numerical versus categorical.
- Numerical variables may be continuous or integer.
- Categorical variables may be ordered or unordered.

Categorical variables
- Some algorithms require categorical variables to be converted to binary dummy variables.
- In regression with R's lm(), factors are dummy-coded internally.
- A full set of dummies should not be included with an intercept in ordinary regression because of perfect multicollinearity.
- Some methods, such as Naive Bayes, can use categorical variables more directly.

Outliers
- An outlier is an observation far from the rest of the data.
- Outliers can have disproportionate influence on models.
- Domain knowledge is needed to decide whether an outlier is an error or a legitimate extreme case.
- In some applications, finding unusual cases is itself the goal; this is anomaly detection.

Missing data
- Many algorithms cannot directly process missing values.
- One option is omission if only a small number of records are affected.
- Another is imputation, replacing missing values with reasonable substitutes.
- Median imputation is one simple example.

Normalisation / standardisation
- Needed when variables on larger numerical scales would dominate distance or optimisation calculations.
- A common z-score standardisation subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation.
- Another approach rescales values to 0-1.
- Distance-based methods such as kNN and clustering are particularly sensitive to scale.

Basic data manipulation and reshaping
- Preliminary work commonly includes selecting variables, filtering records, sorting, creating or recoding variables, grouping records and calculating summaries.
- Grouped summaries can be used to compare counts or numerical measures across categories.
- Data may need to be reshaped before analysis or visualisation.
- The Spell Book demonstrates conversion from wide to long form, where repeated measurement columns are represented by a variable column and a value column.
- Identifier variables should be retained when reshaping so that observations can still be traced correctly.

Preliminary data exploration
- Inspect variable names, dimensions, data types, summary statistics and category frequencies before modelling.
- Explore unusual values, missingness, distributions and relationships between variables.
- Descriptive exploration can reveal data-quality problems and can also suggest useful modelling choices.
- Visualisation is part of exploration rather than merely presentation after a model is fitted.

3. OVERFITTING AND DATA PARTITIONING
============================================================

Overfitting
- A highly complex model can fit training data very well but perform poorly on new data.
- Causes include too many predictors, too many parameters, or trying many alternative models.
- The key concern is predictive performance on unseen data, not merely training fit.

Training partition
- Used to fit or develop the model.

Validation partition
- Used to evaluate the fitted model on data not used to fit it.
- Helps detect overfitting and compare candidate models.

Test partition
- Can be used after repeated model selection on training and validation data.
- Provides a final estimate of performance on unseen data.
- Repeatedly using validation data to choose models or tuning parameters can itself lead to overfitting the validation data.

4. CLASSIFICATION DIAGNOSTICS
============================================================

Confusion matrix outcomes
For a binary classifier:
- True Positive (TP): predicted positive, actually positive.
- True Negative (TN): predicted negative, actually negative.
- False Positive (FP): predicted positive, actually negative.
- False Negative (FN): predicted negative, actually positive.

Sensitivity / recall / true positive rate
Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN)
- Measures the proportion of actual positive cases correctly identified.

Specificity
Specificity = TN / (TN + FP)
- Measures the proportion of actual negative cases correctly identified.

False positive rate
FPR = 1 - Specificity

Accuracy and error rate
Accuracy = correctly classified records / all records.
Error rate = misclassified records / all records.
For multiclass classification, the overall error rate is the sum of all misclassified records divided by the total number of records.

Lecture example with 3,000 records
- TN = 2,689
- FN = 85
- FP = 25
- TP = 201
- Error rate = (25 + 85) / 3000 = 3.67%
- Accuracy = 96.33%
- Sensitivity = 201 / (201 + 85) = 70.28%
- Specificity = 2689 / (2689 + 25) = 99.08%

Probability cutoffs
- A binary classifier often converts predicted probabilities to class labels using a cutoff.
- Changing the cutoff changes TP, TN, FP and FN and therefore changes sensitivity and specificity.
- Lowering the cutoff usually predicts more positives: sensitivity tends to increase while false positives may increase.
- Raising the cutoff usually predicts fewer positives: specificity may increase while sensitivity may decrease.

ROC curve
- ROC stands for Receiver Operating Characteristic.
- The ROC curve plots true positive rate against false positive rate across different probability cutoffs.
- A diagonal line represents random classification.
- Better models generally bow farther towards the upper-left corner.

AUC
- AUC is the area under the ROC curve.
- It summarises how well a model separates the two classes across cutoffs.
- Higher AUC indicates better discrimination.

Cutoff examples
Cutoff 0.50:
- TP = 4, FP = 2, FN = 0, TN = 2
- TPR = 1.00
- FPR = 0.50

Cutoff 0.75:
- TP = 3, FP = 1, FN = 1, TN = 3
- TPR = 0.75
- FPR = 0.25

Cutoff 0.25:
- TP = 4, FP = 3, FN = 0, TN = 1
- TPR = 1.00
- FPR = 0.75

Cumulative gains
- Measures how effectively a model concentrates target cases among records with the highest predicted probabilities.
- Example: selecting the top 30% of cases by predicted probability captured 38% of all target cases.

Cumulative lift
- Compares model-guided selection against selection without a model.
- Lift of 1 means no improvement.
- Example: the top 20% selected by the model produced 1.3 times the target yield of selection without a model.

Response
- Measures the percentage of selected cases that belong to the target class within a ranked group.

Cumulative response
- Measures the target-class response rate when the highest-ranked groups are accumulated.

5. k-NEAREST NEIGHBOURS (kNN)
============================================================

Core idea
- kNN predicts a new record using nearby labelled records.
- For classification, the neighbours vote on the class.
- For numerical prediction, neighbour outcomes can be averaged.
- kNN is a lazy learner: it does not estimate a conventional parametric model before prediction.

Distance
- kNN depends on a distance measure between records.
- Because distance is scale-sensitive, numerical predictors should usually be normalised before fitting and prediction.
- The same transformation learned from training data should be applied to validation and new records.

Choice of k
- k is the number of neighbours consulted.
- Small k creates very local decision boundaries and can be sensitive to individual training records.
- Larger k smooths the decision boundary.
- k can be tuned by comparing predictive metrics across candidate values.

Training and validation
- Model quality should be checked on validation data, not only the training data.
- Confusion matrices can be used to inspect multiclass or binary kNN performance.
- For binary formulations, metrics can include accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 and AUC.

Probabilities
- kNN class probabilities reflect the composition of the selected neighbours.
- With k = 1, a predicted class probability of 100% simply means the single nearest neighbour belongs to that class; it does not imply statistical certainty.

Cutoffs
- When class probabilities are available for a binary target, different probability cutoffs can be evaluated in the same way as other classifiers.

kNN regression
- kNN can also predict a numerical outcome.
- Instead of neighbours voting for a class, the numerical outcomes of nearby training records are combined to form the prediction.
- The same issues of scaling, choice of k, training/validation separation and distance apply.
- Numerical prediction should be evaluated with prediction-error measures such as RMSE or MAE rather than a confusion matrix.

R and Python implementations
- The current Spell Book contains R kNN demonstrations and a Python/Parseltongue counterpart.
- In the Python demonstration, training and validation data are separated before modelling and StandardScaler is fitted from the training predictors before transformation.
- The Python workflow uses scikit-learn tools for preprocessing, partitioning and kNN classification.

6. CROSS-VALIDATION
============================================================

Purpose
- Cross-validation estimates predictive performance more robustly than relying on a single split.
- Data are repeatedly partitioned into training and holdout folds.
- Each fold is used for validation while the remaining folds are used for training.
- Performance is averaged across folds.
- Cross-validation is also useful for tuning model parameters.

Important distinction
- Cross-validation helps with model selection and tuning, but a final independent test set may still be useful when extensive tuning has been performed.

7. LINEAR REGRESSION
============================================================

Basic form
- Linear regression assumes a linear relationship between predictors and a numerical outcome.
- The model contains a constant/intercept, predictor coefficients and an error term.

Explanatory modelling
- Goal: understand relationships between predictors and outcome.
- Common measures include R-squared, residual analysis, p-values and checks of model assumptions.

Predictive modelling
- Goal: predict target values for new observations.
- Fit the model on training data.
- Assess predictive performance on validation data.
- The explanatory role of individual predictors is secondary to predictive accuracy.

Categorical predictors
- R's lm() automatically creates dummy variables for factor predictors.
- With an intercept, one category serves as the reference category.

Prediction workflow
- Choose predictors.
- Explore data.
- Partition into training and validation sets.
- Fit the regression model to training data.
- Predict validation outcomes.
- Assess validation errors.
- Refit the chosen model as appropriate and score new data.

Error metrics
Error = actual - predicted.
- ME: mean error.
- RMSE: root mean squared error.
- MAE: mean absolute error.
- MPE: mean percentage error.
- MAPE: mean absolute percentage error.

Predictor subset selection
- Goal: a parsimonious model that is sufficiently accurate and robust.
- Exhaustive / best subset search evaluates many subsets and can be computationally expensive.
- Forward selection starts with no predictors and adds predictors.
- Backward elimination starts with all predictors and removes predictors.
- Stepwise selection can add and remove predictors during the search.

Transformations
- The current Spell Book includes linear-regression demonstrations with transformed variables, including a machine-learning-oriented version.
- Transformations may be explored when relationships are not adequately represented on the original scale.
- A transformed model should still be evaluated on validation data rather than assumed to be better merely because it is more elaborate.

Model diagnostics
- Regression diagnostics use residuals and related plots or statistics to investigate whether a fitted model behaves as expected.
- Diagnostics may help identify non-linearity, unusual observations, unequal residual variation or other model problems.
- For explanatory modelling, assumption checks are central to interpretation and inference.
- For predictive modelling, validation performance remains essential even when diagnostic checks are also performed.

8. CLASSIFICATION AND REGRESSION TREES
============================================================

Core idea
- Trees classify or predict an outcome using a sequence of rules.
- Also called CART, decision trees, or simply trees.
- Tree diagrams represent the rules graphically.

Recursive partitioning
- Repeatedly split records into two groups.
- Candidate splits are evaluated to increase outcome homogeneity within the resulting groups.
- For classification, purity means that a node contains mostly one class.
- For regression, purity means numerical outcomes within a node are similar.

Tree terminology
- Decision node: a point where the tree splits.
- Terminal node / leaf: an endpoint that produces a prediction.

Classification trees
- Terminal-node class predictions are typically based on the dominant or majority class.
- Impurity measures include Gini and entropy.
- Gini is 0 when a node is completely pure.
- Entropy is also lowest for pure nodes.

Reading rules
- A path from the root to a leaf can be translated into an IF-THEN rule.

Overfitting in trees
- Very large trees can fit noise in the training data.
- Training error may continue falling while validation error eventually rises.
- This is a classic demonstration of overfitting.

Pruning
- A tree can first be grown large and then pruned.
- The aim is to find a simpler tree with better validation performance.
- In rpart, the complexity parameter cp affects how readily the tree grows.
- Smaller cp values permit greater complexity; larger cp values produce simpler trees.

Regression trees
- Used when the outcome is continuous.
- A leaf prediction is typically the average target value among training records in that leaf.
- Impurity can be measured using squared deviations from the leaf mean.
- Predictive performance can be measured by RMSE.

Current Springfield regression-tree demonstration
- Uses fictional Homer Simpson disaster-cost data with numerical and categorical predictors.
- Demonstrates a deliberately large tree, a shallower tree, pruning with the complexity parameter, predictions for new records, improved trees and cross-validation.
- The lesson contrasts training performance with validation performance to illustrate overfitting and pruning.

Advantages of trees
- Easy to understand and interpret.
- Produce explicit decision rules.
- Automatically perform some variable selection.
- Do not rely on the same assumptions as traditional statistical models.

Disadvantages of single trees
- Can be unstable.
- Predictive performance can be weaker than ensemble methods.

Random forests
- Build many trees from bootstrap samples.
- Each tree uses a random subset of predictors.
- Classification predictions are combined by voting.
- Numerical predictions are combined by averaging.
- Often improve predictive performance but reduce interpretability.

Boosting
- Builds trees iteratively.
- Later trees focus more on observations misclassified by earlier trees.
- Predictions are combined using weighted voting or averaging.

9. UNBALANCED CLASS DATA
============================================================

Definition
- Class imbalance occurs when one class represents an overwhelming proportion of a classification dataset.
- This can cause misleading performance if a model mainly predicts the majority class.

Important caveat
- Imbalance is not automatically a defect.
- Domain knowledge matters.
- Fraud is naturally rare, so a transaction dataset may legitimately contain very few fraudulent cases.

Possible remedies
- Rebalancing should generally be performed on the training data rather than contaminating validation data.
- One approach shown in the course uses the ROSE package to generate a more balanced training dataset.
- The model is then trained on the rebalanced training data and evaluated on the untouched validation data.

Evaluation
- Accuracy alone can be misleading with severe imbalance.
- Sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1, ROC/AUC and the practical costs of errors should be considered.

10. CLUSTER ANALYSIS
============================================================

Main idea
- Clustering forms groups of similar records.
- It is unsupervised because there is no target class to predict.
- Applications include customer segmentation, grouping firms, portfolio analysis and scientific classification.

Distance between records
- Euclidean distance is a common measure.
- Distance calculations are strongly influenced by scale.
- Standardisation is therefore usually important.

Hierarchical clustering
Agglomerative:
- Begin with each record as its own cluster.
- Repeatedly merge the closest clusters until one cluster remains.

Divisive:
- Begin with one cluster containing all records.
- Repeatedly divide it.

Dendrogram
- Shows the sequence and distance of hierarchical merges.
- Clusters joined lower on the dendrogram are merged earlier.
- A horizontal cut at a chosen distance determines a possible number of clusters.

Linkage methods
- Single linkage: distance between the closest pair of records across two clusters.
- Complete linkage: distance between the farthest pair.
- Average linkage: average of all pairwise cross-cluster distances.
- Centroid linkage: distance between cluster centroids.
- Ward's method: considers information loss and often produces clusters of roughly similar size.

Non-hierarchical clustering / k-means
- The current Spell Book also includes non-hierarchical clustering.
- k-means requires the analyst to choose the number of clusters k.
- Records are represented in a numerical feature space and assigned to clusters around centroids.
- Because the solution can depend on starting values, multiple starts can be used rather than relying on a single initialisation.
- The elbow method can compare within-cluster sums of squares across candidate values of k.
- The silhouette method provides another way to compare candidate values of k and assess separation.
- Choosing k is not purely mechanical; interpretability and usefulness of the resulting groups also matter.

Cluster validation and interpretation
- Clusters should be meaningful and useful.
- Apparent clusters can occur by chance.
- Different clustering methods can produce different solutions.
- Review descriptive statistics and variables not used for clustering.
- Useful properties include stability and separation.

11. PRINCIPAL COMPONENT ANALYSIS (PCA)
============================================================

Purpose
- PCA reduces a set of numerical variables to a smaller set of principal components.
- Components are combinations of the original variables.
- Components are ordered by the amount of variance they explain.

Scaling
- PCA can be dominated by variables with large numerical scales if raw variables are used.
- Standardising variables before PCA is often appropriate when units or scales differ.

R implementation
- prcomp() can be used for PCA.
- scale. = TRUE standardises variables.
- The rotation matrix contains component loadings.
- The x matrix contains component scores for observations.
- summary() reports standard deviations and proportions of variance explained.

Interpretation
- Loadings show how strongly original variables contribute to each component.
- Scores place observations in the new component space.
- PC1 versus PC2 plots can be used to visualise structure after reduction.

12. NEURAL NETWORKS
============================================================

General concept
- A neural network consists of connected processing units arranged in layers.
- Inputs feed into one or more hidden layers, which then feed into an output layer.
- Connections have weights learned during training.
- Activation functions transform weighted inputs.

Classification
- Neural networks can perform classification.
- A logistic activation function can be used for classification outputs.
- The current classification demonstration explores different hidden-layer structures, including one hidden layer with different numbers of nodes and multiple hidden layers.

Prediction
- Neural networks can also predict continuous numerical outcomes.
- The output and activation choices differ depending on whether the task is classification or numerical prediction.

Model complexity
- More hidden nodes or layers increase model flexibility.
- Greater flexibility can also increase training time and the risk of overfitting.
- Network architecture and other hyperparameters should be evaluated using validation performance.

============================================================
13. NAIVE BAYES
============================================================

General concept
- Naive Bayes is a probabilistic classification method based on Bayes' rule.
- It estimates the probability of each class given the observed predictors.
- The "naive" assumption is that predictors are conditionally independent given the class.
- The method can work surprisingly well even when this assumption is not perfectly satisfied.

Categorical predictors
- Naive Bayes can naturally work with categorical predictors and does not always require the same dummy-variable treatment used by distance-based or regression methods.

Use
- It is primarily a classification method.
- It can be fast and effective for high-dimensional problems such as text classification.

============================================================
14. LOGISTIC REGRESSION
============================================================

Purpose
- Logistic regression is used for classification when the outcome is categorical, commonly binary.
- A fitted binary logistic model can produce an estimated probability for the positive class.
- In R, the Spell Book demonstrates logistic regression using a binomial generalised linear model and caret-based workflows.

From probabilities to classes
- Predicted probabilities can be converted to class labels using a cutoff.
- A cutoff of 0.50 is a common starting point but is not automatically optimal.
- Changing the cutoff changes the confusion matrix and the balance between sensitivity and specificity.
- One Spell Book demonstration also illustrates selection of a cutoff using Youden's index, which seeks a favourable combination of sensitivity and specificity.

Evaluation
- Evaluate logistic regression on validation data rather than relying only on the fitted training model.
- Useful measures include the confusion matrix, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 and ROC/AUC.
- Gains, lift, response and cumulative response plots can also be used when ranked probability scores matter.

Prediction
- Logistic regression can be used to score new records after the model has been selected and evaluated.
- Keep the distinction between the predicted probability and the final class produced after applying a cutoff.

============================================================
15. DISCRIMINANT ANALYSIS
============================================================

General concept
- Discriminant analysis is a supervised classification method.
- The current Spell Book demonstrates classification with a training/validation split and also demonstrates posterior class probabilities.
- Linear discriminant analysis can be fitted using class-labelled training records and numerical predictors.

Probabilities and prediction
- A fitted discriminant model can return posterior probabilities for each possible class.
- The predicted class is related to these class probabilities rather than being an unexplained label.
- Validation data should be used to evaluate performance on records that were not used to fit the model.

16. ASSOCIATION RULES AND MARKET-BASKET ANALYSIS
============================================================

General concept
- Association rules look for combinations of items or events that occur together.
- Market-basket analysis is a common application, but the same logic can be applied to other transaction-style data.
- Data are represented as transactions containing items.

Rule structure
- A rule has an antecedent on the left-hand side and a consequent on the right-hand side.
- The Apriori algorithm can generate candidate rules subject to minimum support and confidence thresholds.

Support
- Support measures how frequently the complete rule occurs in the transaction data.
- A very rare rule can have high confidence but still be of limited practical use, so support matters.

Confidence
- Confidence measures how often the consequent appears among transactions containing the antecedent.
- It can be read as a conditional success rate for the rule.

Lift
- Lift compares the observed co-occurrence with what would be expected without the association.
- Lift equal to 1 indicates no improvement over independence.
- Lift greater than 1 indicates a positive association; larger values indicate a stronger departure from independence.
- Rules can be sorted by lift, but interpretation should still consider support, confidence and practical usefulness.

17. COLLABORATIVE FILTERING AND RECOMMENDER SYSTEMS
============================================================

Ratings data
- Collaborative filtering can use a user-by-item rating matrix containing observed ratings and missing entries for items not yet rated.
- Similarity between users or items can be calculated from the available ratings.
- The current Spell Book demonstration uses cosine similarity.

Item-based collaborative filtering
- The current demonstration uses item-based collaborative filtering (IBCF).
- The recommender learns from the observed rating matrix and predicts ratings for currently unrated items.
- Predicted ratings can then be used to produce a top-N recommendation.

Interpretation
- A recommendation is based on patterns in the ratings data; it is not evidence that the recommended item is objectively best.
- Sparse ratings and limited overlap between users or items can make similarity and recommendations less stable.

18. TEXT ANALYTICS AND TEXT CLUSTERING
============================================================

Text preparation
- Text analytics usually requires substantial preprocessing before modelling.
- The Spell Book demonstrates converting text to lower case, removing numbers, removing common and custom stop words, removing punctuation, stripping extra white space and stemming words to root forms.
- Text can be organised as a corpus and converted into a term-document representation.

Frequency analysis
- Word counts can identify frequently occurring terms.
- Frequent words can be displayed in tables, bar charts or word clouds.
- Frequency alone does not establish meaning, importance or sentiment; interpretation still matters.

Sentiment analysis
- The Spell Book demonstrates assigning sentiment categories to words and aggregating those results for exploratory sentiment analysis.
- Sentiment dictionaries are a rule-based representation of language and should not be treated as perfect understanding of context.

Text clustering
- Text records can be converted into numerical representations and then clustered.
- The current demonstrations use k-means clustering and compare candidate values of k with elbow and silhouette methods.
- Cluster interpretation can use frequent words and cluster-specific TF-IDF-style summaries to identify distinguishing terms.
- Analysts can assign descriptive labels to clusters only after examining what the clusters contain.

Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA)
- Another Spell Book demonstration uses Latent Semantic Analysis before clustering.
- LSA reduces a high-dimensional word-document representation into a smaller semantic space.
- Documents using different vocabulary but discussing similar themes may become closer in that reduced space.
- k-means can then be applied to the reduced LSA representation.

21. MODEL-SELECTION PRINCIPLES
============================================================

No single metric or algorithm is universally best.
When comparing models:
- Use validation or cross-validation performance.
- Choose metrics that reflect the business or analytical objective.
- Consider the relative cost of false positives and false negatives.
- Consider interpretability, stability and computational cost.
- Be cautious about training performance that is much better than validation performance.

22. EXCEL AND SPREADSHEET-BASED BUSINESS ANALYTICS
============================================================

Spreadsheet basics
- Excel is used as an analytical modelling environment as well as a calculation tool.
- Formulas may combine cell references, operators and functions.
- Relative references change when formulas are copied.
- Absolute references, such as $B$4, remain fixed when copied.
- Mixed references fix either the row or the column.
- Common functions include SUM, AVERAGE, MIN, MAX and COUNT-style functions.
- IF and other logical functions implement business rules based on conditions.

Lookup functions
- VLOOKUP retrieves a value from a lookup table based on a key in the first column of the lookup range.
- INDEX returns a value from a specified position in a range.
- MATCH returns the position of a lookup value.
- INDEX and MATCH can be combined for flexible lookups.
- Course examples also combine VLOOKUP with IF or MATCH.
- Exact versus approximate matching should be chosen deliberately.

Data visualisation
- Visualisation is used to identify patterns that may be difficult to see in tables alone.
- Excel chart types covered include column/bar, line, pie, area, scatter and bubble charts.
- Scatter plots are useful for examining relationships between two numerical variables.
- Conditional formatting can highlight patterns, thresholds and unusual values.
- Sparklines provide compact trend displays.
- Dashboards combine multiple relevant displays to support monitoring and decision making.
- The Excel Camera tool can support linked dashboard displays.

Tables, sorting and filtering
- Excel tables provide structured ranges for sorting, filtering and calculations.
- Sorting reorganises records by one or more fields.
- Filtering displays records meeting specified criteria without deleting the other records.
- Pareto analysis can focus attention on the relatively small number of categories accounting for a large share of an outcome.

Frequency distributions and PivotTables
- Frequency distributions summarise how often values or categories occur.
- Relative frequency expresses counts as proportions or percentages.
- Histograms group numerical data into bins; bin choices affect interpretation.
- Cumulative relative frequency summarises the proportion of observations at or below successive values.
- Percentiles and quartiles describe relative position in a distribution.
- Cross-tabulations summarise relationships between categorical variables.
- PivotTables aggregate and reorganise data interactively.
- PivotCharts visualise PivotTable summaries.
- Slicers provide interactive filtering controls.
- PivotTables, PivotCharts and slicers can be combined into dashboards.

Descriptive statistics
- Measures of location include mean, median, mode and midrange.
- Measures of dispersion include range, interquartile range, variance and standard deviation.
- Coefficient of variation expresses standard deviation relative to the mean.
- Skewness describes asymmetry; kurtosis describes aspects of distribution shape/tail behaviour.
- z-scores express values in standard-deviation units relative to the mean.
- Chebyshev's theorem provides distribution-free bounds on the proportion of observations within a given number of standard deviations.
- The empirical rule gives approximate 68/95/99.7% coverage for a roughly normal distribution.
- The Excel Descriptive Statistics tool produces multiple summary measures.
- Covariance and correlation describe relationships between numerical variables.
- Correlation measures linear association and does not establish causation.
- Outliers should be investigated rather than automatically removed.
- Sample statistics vary from sample to sample.

Statistical inference
- Hypothesis testing compares sample evidence with a null hypothesis.
- The alternative hypothesis and one- or two-tailed form depend on the analytical question.
- A test statistic measures how far the observed sample result is from what is expected under the null hypothesis.
- A p-value indicates how unusual the observed result would be if the null hypothesis were true.
- Type I error is rejecting a true null hypothesis.
- Type II error is failing to reject a false null hypothesis.
- Test power is the probability of detecting an effect when the relevant alternative is true.
- Procedures covered include one-sample tests for means/proportions, two-sample tests for means, paired tests, F-tests for equality of variances, ANOVA and chi-square tests for independence.
- Excel Analysis ToolPak procedures are used for several tests; choosing and interpreting the appropriate procedure is part of the analysis.

Trendlines and regression in Excel
- Excel trendlines model relationships or trends; possible forms include linear and nonlinear models.
- R-squared summarises the proportion of variation explained by a fitted regression model but does not by itself establish model quality.
- Polynomial models can overfit and can behave poorly when extrapolated.
- Simple linear regression models a numerical outcome using one predictor.
- Least squares chooses coefficients by minimising the sum of squared residuals.
- Residuals are observed outcomes minus fitted/predicted outcomes.
- Regression output includes fit statistics, ANOVA information, coefficient estimates, hypothesis tests and confidence intervals.
- Residual analysis is used to investigate modelling assumptions.
- Multiple regression uses two or more predictors.
- Model building should be systematic rather than based only on in-sample fit.
- Multicollinearity occurs when predictors contain strongly overlapping information.
- Overfitting occurs when a model fits sample-specific noise and performs poorly on new data.
- Categorical variables, interaction terms and nonlinear terms can be incorporated where appropriate.

Linear optimisation and Solver
- A linear optimisation model contains decision variables, an objective function and constraints.
- The objective can maximise or minimise an outcome such as profit, return or cost.
- Constraints represent resource limits or business rules.
- Decision-variable cells hold quantities that Solver may change.
- The objective cell calculates the quantity to maximise/minimise.
- SUMPRODUCT is useful for linear totals such as profit or resource use.
- Excel Solver can optimise the model subject to constraints; Simplex LP is appropriate for linear models.
- Solver Answer Reports summarise objective values, variable values and constraint status.
- Sensitivity Reports for linear models can provide reduced costs, shadow prices and allowable changes.
- Supplied demonstrations include Sklenka Skis (product mix), an investment-allocation problem and a HotSprings Spas production problem.

Why information systems matter

Computer-based information systems

Types of organisational systems

24. DATABASES, DATA MODELLING AND SQL
============================================================

Database foundations
- Data are raw facts; information is data organised or processed to provide meaning.
- Database systems provide structured, shared storage and management.
- A DBMS manages structures and access rather than leaving each application to maintain isolated files.
- Traditional file systems can suffer from redundancy, inconsistency, data/structural dependence and update/insertion/deletion anomalies.
- Good database design improves integrity, sharing, security and maintainability.

Relational model
- Tables represent data in rows and columns.
- Rows/tuples represent records; columns/attributes represent properties.
- A primary key uniquely identifies a row.
- Candidate keys are possible unique identifiers; one is selected as the primary key.
- Foreign keys reference keys in other tables and implement relationships.
- Functional dependencies describe how one attribute/set of attributes determines another.
- Entity integrity and referential integrity protect consistency.
- A data dictionary/system catalogue stores metadata.
- Indexes can improve retrieval performance but add storage/maintenance trade-offs.

Entity-relationship modelling
- Entities represent distinguishable objects/concepts.
- Attributes describe entities.
- Relationships describe associations between entities.
- Connectivity includes one-to-one, one-to-many and many-to-many forms.
- Cardinality expresses numerical relationship constraints.
- Existence dependence and participation express whether related occurrences are optional or required.
- Many-to-many relationships are commonly resolved with an associative/composite entity.
- ERD development involves identifying entities, attributes, identifiers, relationships, connectivity, cardinality and participation, then checking the model against business rules.
- Supplied materials demonstrate ER modelling in Microsoft Visio and Lucidchart.

Normalisation
- Normalisation reduces avoidable redundancy and update anomalies.
- First normal form (1NF) removes repeating groups/multivalued structures and establishes atomic values for the chosen model.
- Second normal form (2NF) removes partial dependencies on part of a composite key.
- Third normal form (3NF) removes transitive dependencies between non-key attributes.
- Surrogate keys may be introduced where appropriate.
- Denormalisation deliberately introduces selected redundancy for performance/practical reasons and should be a conscious design trade-off.

SQL foundations
- SQL can function as a data manipulation language (DML), data definition language (DDL), transaction control language (TCL) and data control language (DCL).
- SQL is declarative/nonprocedural: the query specifies the result rather than a procedural algorithm.

SELECT query structure
- SELECT chooses columns/expressions to return.
- FROM identifies source tables.
- WHERE filters individual rows.
- GROUP BY forms groups.
- HAVING filters groups after aggregation.
- ORDER BY sorts the final result.
- DISTINCT removes duplicate result rows where appropriate.
- Comparison operators include =, <, <=, >, >= and not-equal forms.
- Logical operators include AND, OR and NOT.
- Special operators include BETWEEN, IN, LIKE and IS NULL.

Joins
- Inner joins return matching rows.
- Outer joins retain matches plus unmatched rows depending on left, right or full join.
- NATURAL JOIN relies on same-named compatible columns.
- JOIN ... USING specifies a shared column.
- JOIN ... ON specifies an explicit condition.
- CROSS JOIN produces a Cartesian product.
- Table aliases improve clarity in multi-table queries.

Aggregation
- COUNT, MIN, MAX, SUM and AVG are common aggregate functions.
- GROUP BY combines aggregation with categories/groups.
- HAVING applies conditions to grouped results.
- Good query construction starts with understanding the data model and the business question.

Creating/changing data
- CREATE TABLE defines table structure, data types and constraints.
- Constraints covered include PRIMARY KEY, FOREIGN KEY, NOT NULL, UNIQUE, DEFAULT and CHECK.
- INSERT adds rows; INSERT ... SELECT can add rows from a query.
- UPDATE changes existing rows.
- DELETE removes rows.
- ROLLBACK can undo uncommitted changes in supported transaction contexts.
- SQL dialects differ, so syntax may vary by DBMS.

25. CYBERSECURITY AND ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE
============================================================

AI/ML foundations
- Supervised learning uses labelled outcomes and includes classification and numerical prediction.
- Unsupervised learning seeks patterns without a target and includes clustering, association rules and data reduction.
- Training and validation should be separated to evaluate performance on data not used to fit a model.
- Modelling risks include overfitting, poor data quality and weak evaluation design.

Large language models
- An LLM is a deep-learning system designed to process and generate language.
- Transformers are a foundational architecture; self-attention relates tokens across a sequence.
- Pre-training learns statistical patterns from large text corpora.
- Fine-tuning/adaptation can specialise a model.
- Inference is the stage in which a trained model responds to new input.
- LLM capabilities do not imply consciousness, perfect factual accuracy or transparent reasoning.

Turing Test
- The Turing Test evaluates whether machine conversation can be indistinguishable from human conversation under test conditions.
- It is historically important but has limitations: imitating conversation is not equivalent to demonstrating consciousness, broad intelligence or reliable reasoning.
- The supplied teaching material asks, with a Star Wars reference, whether machines can "master the ways of the force".

AI-driven phishing
- Phishing impersonates a legitimate entity/person to trick targets into revealing sensitive information or taking harmful actions.
- Generative AI can improve personalisation, fluency, speed and scale.
- AI-generated phishing may contain fewer traditional grammar/spelling warning signs.
- Defence requires technical controls, user awareness, verification and adaptive detection rather than a single clue.

Data poisoning
- Data poisoning introduces malicious/misleading data into AI training data to influence model behaviour.
- Targeted attacks seek to alter particular outputs.
- Indiscriminate attacks seek to degrade overall performance/reliability.
- Examples include attacks on content moderation, malware detection and generative-AI systems.
- Defences include data validation/provenance, monitoring/auditing, robust/adversarial training where appropriate, and threat-intelligence sharing.

AI and malware detection
- AI/ML can learn patterns associated with normal and malicious activity.
- It can support faster and near-real-time detection/prevention.
- Detection may use behavioural or file-related features rather than only known signatures.
- The supplied material notes emerging techniques that convert file attributes into visual representations for image-based detection.

Ethics, privacy and accountability
- AI-driven cybersecurity systems can reproduce or amplify bias.
- Security monitoring can conflict with privacy; more data access is not automatically justified merely because it may improve detection.
- Accountability can involve developers, deployers, organisations and human decision-makers.
- Human oversight is important for consequential security decisions.
- Offensive AI/cyberwarfare raises questions about escalation, proportionality, attribution and international governance.

Transparency and law: supplied course examples
- A 2025 ethics/law handout uses explainability and transparency as examples of AI governance challenges.
- It discusses the EU AI Act and selected 2024 U.S. state laws/bills involving AI transparency, disclosure and training-data requirements.
- These are course examples, not current legal advice; laws, effective dates and interpretations can change.
